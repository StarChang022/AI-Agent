# You don't need to be an all-around investor, just stay in your circle of competence

When people first start investing, they often want to act like an all-knowing expert who can dish out unique insights on every hot tech stock or trending industry. But in the real world of investing, trying to understand every company in the market is usually how you end up losing money. Warren Buffett and his legendary partner Charlie Munger have hammered this point home for decades: the key to investment success is staying squarely inside your circle of competence.

You don't have to understand everything. Focus on companies you truly get, much like a baseball batter patiently waiting for a pitch right down the middle to score steady, long-term returns. And when a business goes over your head, being honest with yourself and stepping back is the single best way to protect your hard-earned cash.

## There are no strikeouts in investing: Patiently wait for your sweet spot

In baseball, batters face intense pressure. The umpire calls strikes and balls on every pitch, and if you watch too many good strikes go by, you're called out on strikes.

Investing is a lot like baseball in that you need to hit the ball to score returns. But financial markets don't have an umpire calling you out. You can let hundreds of pitches pass by until you get the one right in your sweet spot.

### The science of hitting and the 77-cell grid

Ted Williams, the legendary hitter and the only player in baseball history to hit .400 in a single season, shared his secret in his book *The Science of Hitting*. Williams divided the strike zone into a grid of 77 baseballs. He realized that swinging only at pitches right in the middle of his "sweet spot" yielded a .400 batting average. Reaching for pitches on the edges dragged his average down to .230. His success was all about waiting strictly for his pitch.

### The absolute advantage of financial markets

The stock market is basically a ballgame with no strikeout rule. No umpire will ever call you out for watching 100 pitches pass. You can stay in the batter's box and watch hundreds of companies fly past every day. No matter how loud online gurus or TV pundits yell, if the pitch doesn't land right in the middle of your circle of competence, you don't have to swing. The main reason everyday investors lose money is seeing someone else hit a home run on a trending stock or complex financial product, getting anxious, and swinging blindly at pitches they can't even see clearly.

## What is a true circle of competence? Don't confuse consumer habit with an economic moat

If you want to swing only at sweet-spot pitches in the stock market, your first step is defining what a true circle of competence really is, and calling out the myth of "I understand this company."

Drinking Starbucks every day, holding the latest iPhone, or driving a Tesla doesn't mean those companies are in your circle of competence. That's just consumer experience, not a business circle of competence.

Peter Lynch famously said you can spot great businesses in your daily life. And he was right! But finding a hidden gem is just the start. You still have to dig deep into their business model, financial statements, cost structure, supply chain bargaining power, return on equity (ROE), and how resilient they are against tough competitors.

In short, being a fan of a product isn't the same as having an investment circle of competence. You still need to do the homework.

## The size of your circle doesn't matter, but its boundary does

In investing, winning doesn't depend on how broad your knowledge base is. Warren Buffett famously put it this way: "You don't need to be an expert on every company, or even on tech companies. You only have to be able to evaluate companies within your circle. How large that circle is isn't very important, but knowing where its boundaries are is vital."

### The power of admitting "I don't know"

Charlie Munger stressed that knowing what you don't know is far more useful than trying to be brilliant.

The most dangerous trap in investing is thinking you know something when you don't. When faced with a business that's hard to value, has a super complex business model, or operates in a fast-changing tech space, top investors won't hesitate to toss it into the "too hard" pile. Saying "I don't understand this" isn't a sign of weakness; it's your strongest defense mechanism to protect your money.

### Overcoming fear of missing out (FOMO)

When coworkers or friends strike it rich short term by speculating on hot biotech stocks, crypto, or complex financial gadgets, it puts your discipline to the test. That FOMO temptation tries to pull you right out of your comfort zone.

The key to staying calm is realizing that someone else's home run has nothing to do with you. At the end of the day, a home run hit from waiting for your sweet spot pitch yields the exact same return.

## A 3-step self-test to see if you're really inside your circle

To avoid confusing a feeling with actual capability, you need an objective, practical check. Before putting your hard-earned cash into any stock, run through this self-test to verify whether the business actually sits inside your circle of competence.

### Step 1: The simple English test

Can you explain how this company makes money in 5 minutes using plain English that a 12-year-old could understand, to someone with zero finance background?

When you can break down the business model and why customers buy in plain language, it shows you genuinely get how the company turns a profit. On top of that, you should know where its revenue comes from, what its main costs are, and what its profit margins look like.

On the flip side, if your explanation is loaded with heavy jargon, unproven tech patents, or complex financial structures you can barely explain yourself, it's a huge red flag that you've stepped outside your circle.

### Step 2: The stress test

How do you react mentally and strategically if this company's stock drops 30% out of nowhere?

Can you calmly check financial reports and industry trends to figure out whether the dip is just short-term market noise or a structural shift in the core business model, and decide whether to buy the dip?

If you panic and resort to frantically refreshing forums or asking influencers why it's dropping, you don't actually understand the company's intrinsic value, you're just blindly following the crowd.

### Step 3: The longevity test

Industries move at different speeds, but here's what matters: can you say with confidence that this company or industry will still be around 10 years from now?

If you're truly inside your circle of competence, you'll know the answer.

## How to safely expand your circle of competence

Staying inside your circle doesn't mean sticking to your old ways forever or refusing to learn new things. Over time, you can gradually expand your scope, but doing so takes extreme caution. You can't just take wild leaps.

1. **The concentric circle approach**: Start learning from what you already know and build outward. For example, if you work at a semiconductor equipment company, equipment is your core circle. From there, you can slowly branch out into upstream material suppliers or downstream packaging and testing plants.
2. **Dig deep into primary sources**: Skip second-hand news headlines and short social media clips. Make it a habit to read annual reports (10-K), quarterly reports (10-Q), and authoritative industry whitepapers directly. Getting first-hand data and raw information is the only way to build real business insight.
3. **Set a strict observation period**: Don't jump right in with real money when looking at a new sector or company. Create a watchlist and track their operations and financial performance for a year or two. Allocate capital only when you fully grasp the business logic and key risks.

## Takeaways from Mr.Cafe

Investing is a marathon, and medals at the finish line go to those who protect their capital and let their wealth compound over time, not to whoever tried the hardest tricks.

Staying inside your circle of competence won't make you rich overnight, but it will keep you from hitting catastrophic landmines, helping you sleep soundly while steadily building financial freedom.

1. **Boundaries matter more than size**: Knowing what you don't know is far more important than trying to figure out every company on the market.
2. **Consumer experience isn't competence**: Don't assume you understand a business model just because you use its products or services. There's a massive difference.
3. **Run the 3-step test every time**: Before risking your money, always run the simple English test, stress test, and longevity test.
4. **Stay patient**: Tune out market noise and FOMO, and wait patiently for pitches that land squarely in your sweet spot.
