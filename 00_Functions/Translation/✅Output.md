# Facebook

[Two companies in the same industry making the exact same cash. Why is one worth 10x more per share 20 years later?]

Picture two direct competitors. Over the past twenty years, their revenue growth and operating profits have been identical, with each spinning off billions in free cash flow every single year.

Company A's CEO blew that cash on overpriced acquisitions of buzzy startups, bought back company stock at all-time highs just to offset executive stock option dilution, and spent a small fortune building a flashy glass-tower headquarters.
Company B's CEO did the exact opposite: aggressively repurchased and retired 80% of outstanding shares during brutal bear markets, kept internal R&D strictly focused on its high-ROE core business, and paid out any leftover cash to shareholders as dividends.

Twenty years in, Company A's intrinsic value per share has barely moved, while Company B's value per share has surged more than tenfold.

Warren Buffett has spent decades pointing out a simple truth in his annual letters: the moment someone becomes CEO, they essentially become the fund manager of your personal money. Most CEOs climb the corporate ladder because they're great at sales, marketing, engineering, or legal work, not because they know how to allocate capital. An operational rockstar can spend ten years shaving millions off production costs, only to incinerate five years of saved cash over a single weekend by overpaying for a bad acquisition.

To see whether a CEO is actually doing a good job with your money, Buffett uses three straightforward checks:

1. The five choices for free cash flow:
After funding the basic CapEx needed to stay competitive, a CEO only has five places to put spare cash: reinvest in the core business, acquire other companies, pay down debt, pay dividends, or buy back shares. Great managers weigh the opportunity cost of every single option and funnel each dollar toward whatever drives the highest per-share return.

2. The "$1 retained earnings test":
For every dollar of earnings a company holds back instead of paying out, does it create at least one dollar of market value over a rolling five-year period? If it doesn't, retaining earnings is literally turning shareholder gold into lead.

3. Seeing through the buyback illusion:
Stock buybacks aren't always good. Everything depends on whether the buyback price is below intrinsic value. As Buffett says, buying dollar bills for eighty cents or less creates massive value for shareholders. But buying back stock at sky-high valuations usually just props up quarterly EPS to trigger executive bonuses or quietly hides dilution from stock-based compensation.

When you buy a stock, you're placing your financial future in the hands of the person in the driver's seat deciding where that cash goes.
